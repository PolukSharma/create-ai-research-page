CREATE Research on Artificial Intelligence

Securing Critical Infrastructure in the Age of AI

For more than two decades, CREATE has helped the nation understand what threats to critical infrastructure cost in lives, economic activity, and resilience. As AI moves into electric power, transportation, water, communications, ports, and supply chains, CREATE is developing the evidence, methods, and public-policy frameworks needed to deploy AI securely—and to prevent unacceptable systemic consequences.

$110B

Potential annual savings for power plants from widespread use of existing AI applications—plus 175 GW of unlocked transmission capacity by 2035.

International Energy Agency estimate · Potential gains and AI failure risks remain largely unquantified

Distinctive contribution

Consequence-informed AI security and resilience

CREATE connects decisions about AI deployment, defense, autonomy, and human oversight to quantitative models of societal and economic consequence—assessing whether AI can perform safely, how adversaries might exploit it, and where AI-enabled defenses can reduce risk.

Integrated research agenda

Three interrelated challenges

Protecting infrastructure from AI-enabled attack, using AI defensively, and keeping embedded AI reliable and controllable—treated together because infrastructure systems are interconnected.

Challenge 01

Defend against AI-enabled adversaries

Protect infrastructure from adversaries who use AI to discover vulnerabilities, evade defenses, manipulate operational data, or coordinate cyber-physical attacks.

Challenge 02

Use AI defensively

Detect intrusions, anticipate equipment failures, and support response to wildfire, extreme weather, and other hazards before essential services are lost.

Challenge 03

Keep embedded AI reliable and controllable

Ensure AI embedded in infrastructure remains trustworthy, resistant to manipulation, and bounded by clear thresholds for human intervention and autonomous action.

Systems view

Why interconnection matters

An attack, compromised AI agent, or physical disruption in one network can propagate across others. CREATE studies how cascading failures spread—and how they can be detected, isolated, and contained.

Cascading risk across interdependent sectors—and the AI systems increasingly embedded in each.

From research to national capability

Testing AI before it is entrusted with critical infrastructure

CREATE will turn this research into an independent capability for evaluating AI under realistic stress— so operators and regulators can decide where AI can be deployed, what authority it should receive, and when human intervention must be retained.

  1. 1

    Simulations & testbeds

    Reproduce AI-enabled attacks, equipment failures, extreme-weather events, and cascading disruptions at infrastructure scale.

  2. 2

    Government & industry demos

    Work with partners to show whether AI-enabled defenses can detect and contain failures before essential services are lost.

  3. 3

    Practical evaluation methods

    Give operators and regulators clear criteria for deployment authority, oversight, and human-in-the-loop thresholds.

CREATE AI Research Fellows

BB

Burçin Becerik-Gerber

Professor & Chair, Civil & Environmental Engineering · USC

Built environments Emergency response Human-centered AI

Human-centered AI for design, operation, and resilience of buildings—especially emergency preparedness and response.

Read more

Advances human-centered artificial intelligence for the design, operation, and resilience of complex built environments, with a particular focus on emergency preparedness and response. She develops AI-driven sensing, simulation, and decision-support systems that integrate human behavior, building performance, and environmental conditions to better anticipate and manage high-risk scenarios such as active threats, evacuations, and extreme events. This work combines virtual reality, agent-based modeling, and data-driven methods to evaluate how design and operational decisions influence safety, health, and human outcomes under uncertainty. By grounding AI in real human experience and spatial context, this research aims to inform evidence-based design, training, and policy strategies that enhance resilience and reduce risk in buildings and communities (Liu, Becerik-Gerber, et al., 2025).

BD

Bistra Dilkina

Associate Professor, Computer Science & ISE · Co-Director, CAIS · USC

Optimization Machine learning Sustainability

Machine-learning-driven combinatorial optimization for large-scale sustainability and planning problems.

Read more

Focuses on advancing the state of the art in combinatorial optimization techniques for solving real-world large-scale problems, particularly ones that arise in sustainability areas such as biodiversity conservation planning and urban planning. Her work is at the intersection of discrete optimization and machine learning. One key area is designing machine-learning-driven combinatorial optimization algorithms, by leveraging the plethora of data generated by solving distributions of real-world optimization problems (Huang, Piansky, Dilkina, & Molzahn, 2025; Pynadath, Dilkina, Jeong, John, et al., 2023).

RJ

Richard John

Professor of Psychology · Senior Research Fellow · USC

Security games Behavioral models Resource allocation

Algorithms that allocate security resources against adaptive adversaries, informed by behavioral game experiments.

Read more

Has developed and tested algorithms to optimize resource allocation, minimizing the threat, vulnerability, and expected consequences of attacks by adaptive adversaries (Cui & John, 2014; Pita, John, Maheswaran, Tambe, et al., 2012; Yang, Kiekintveld, Ordonez, Tambe, & John, 2013). These algorithms utilize a Stackelberg Security Game framework and account for the adversary’s risk attitude, uncertainty, and conflicting objectives. In addition, the algorithms are designed to relax the usual assumption of a rational adversary and account for heuristics and biases that have been empirically validated in behavioral game experiments. Example events modeled include recovery from a biological attack in a major U.S. city (Pynadath, Rosoff, & John, 2016) and public evacuation response during a hurricane warning (Pynadath, Dilkina, Jeong, John, et al., 2023). These agent-based models are informed by empirical behavioral data collected from surveys and immersive virtual simulation experiments.

KS

Ketan Savla

Associate Professor, Civil & Environmental Engineering · USC

Network resilience Adaptation Peer influence

Adaptation protocols and algorithms for resilient interconnected infrastructure under disruption.

Read more

With his research team, has provided quantitative insights into the optimal adaptation protocols for resilience in interconnected systems, such as civil infrastructure. His work considers physics, system connectivity, and behavioral constraints (Savla, Shamma, & Dahleh, 2020). He has also synthesized, and empirically investigated the efficacy of, algorithms that utilize information asymmetry and the power of peer influence to nudge individual decisions towards social good (Zhu & Savla, 2022). These works collectively provide foundations for an AI system to enable complex systems to adapt, from individual-level to systems-level, towards a variety of disruptions.

SC

Samrat Chatterjee

Chief Data Scientist · Pacific Northwest National Laboratory

Critical infrastructure RL agents Cyber-physical

Safety-critical generative AI and reinforcement-learning agents for cyber and cyber-physical infrastructure.

Read more

Over the past three years has focused on two key areas of safety-critical Generative AI settings for critical infrastructure: (1) understanding infrastructure risk, resilience, and AI connections, and (2) developing robust and secure AI agents based on Reinforcement learning (RL) involving sequential decision making in critical cyber and cyber-physical system settings. While initial progress is promising, more work remains to fully realize the potential for advanced levels of autonomy via AI systems within real-world critical infrastructure operations. (Du, Chatterjee, et al., 2023; Dutta, Chatterjee, et al., 2023; Mukherjee, Chatterjee, et al., 2026; Rahman, Shuvo, Chatterjee, et al., 2025; Rose, Prager, Chen, & Chatterjee, 2017; Thekdi, Tatar, Santos, & Chatterjee, 2023; Thekdi, Tatar, Santos, & Chatterjee, 2024)

VB

Vicki Bier

Professor Emeritus · University of Wisconsin–Madison

AI safety Human/AI learning LLM security

AI safety and human/AI collaboration—comparing human and machine learning, and strategies for safer LLMs.

Read more

Has been studying issues related to AI safety and human/AI collaboration. With regard to the former, funding from UW and the National Science Foundation supported work on the relative strengths and weaknesses of human and machine learning. This project built a platform that allowed humans and AI systems to play a set of rule-learning games on an equal basis. Subsequently, Pulick et al. (2022; 2023) presented results of experiments conducted on this platform, expanding for example on the challenges posed by rule generality for human learners, since humans appear to rely on assumptions about possible simple rules, which may complicate learning of rules that don’t fit those schema. With regard to AI safety, Gutfraind and Bier (2023; 2025) highlighted a number of strategies that could be used to help improve the safety and security of large learning models.

JZ

Jun Zhuang

Morton C. Frank Professor of Industrial & Systems Engineering

Homeland security Disaster management Agentic AI

Data-driven and decision-centric AI for homeland security, crisis misinformation, and resource allocation.

Read more

Focuses on advancing data-driven and decision-centric methods for homeland security and disaster management. His prior research applied machine learning to complex security problems, including predicting unauthorized immigration flows using nonparametric models that integrate economic, demographic, climatic, and border security factors, and developing AI frameworks to monitor and classify misinformation on social media during crisis events, enabling more effective situational awareness and resource allocation (Al Aziz, Ahmed, & Zhuang, 2024; Hunt, Agarwal, & Zhuang, 2022). Building on this foundation, his ongoing and future research aims to integrate game theory, multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA), and Agentic AI to support adaptive, explainable, and strategically informed resource allocation in homeland security and disaster response, particularly in complex, uncertain, and high-risk environments such as the Arctic.

AR

Adam Rose

Research Professor · CREATE Director Emeritus · USC

Economic consequence Resilience Supply chains

Machine learning and agent-based models for economic consequences of disasters and supply-chain resilience.

Read more

Research Professor of Public Policy and of Civil and Environmental Engineering, and CREATE Director Emeritus, recently served on a panel at the Symposium on Artificial Intelligence and Public Safety sponsored by the Israeli Consulate of Los Angeles. Professor Rose commented on potential contributions of AI to analyzing the economic consequences of and resilience to man-made and natural disasters. Professor Rose’s research team utilized machine learning in developing the reduced form decision-support system known as the Economic Consequence Analysis Tool (E-CAT) (Rose et al., 2017). He is currently working on a Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) project, in conjunction with Noah Dormady, Associate Professor in the John Glenn College of Public Affairs, integrating resilience (Dormady, Rose, et al., 2022) into agent-based models of critical supply chain resilience on the basis of empirical research production function modeling on a project sponsored by DARPA.
